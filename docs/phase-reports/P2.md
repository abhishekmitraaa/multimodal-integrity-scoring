Date: 2026-10-24. Status: **approved by the lead (2026-10-24).** The human real-keyboard check stays
open and runs alongside P3. Online scoring rule: prototype (D-033). Full result tables:

## Headline

**The TypeNet reproduction passes: all 10 Table 2 cells (M = 50 and 70, G = 1–10, triplet loss)
fall inside their acceptance bands.** This is on D1 (Aalto 136M Keystrokes), with the paper's
protocol and the first 1,000 test subjects, scored with Eq. 5. One documented deviation: the
learning rate is 0.005, not the paper's 0.05.

| M | G | Ours (mean per-subject EER) | TypeNet | Band | 5 random draws of 1,000 subjects |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 70 | 1 | 4.11% | 4.5% | [2.25, 6.75] | 4.56% ± 0.31 |
| 70 | 5 | 1.57% | 1.7% | [0.70, 2.70] | 1.72% ± 0.08 |
| 70 | 10 | 1.17% | 1.2% | [0.20, 2.20] | 1.18% ± 0.10 |
| 50 | 1 | 4.14% | 5.4% | [2.70, 8.10] | 4.68% ± 0.17 |
| 50 | 5 | 1.55% | 2.2% | [1.10, 3.30] | 1.82% ± 0.07 |

The LSTM's EER is about 15–20 times lower than either non-deep baseline on the same subjects
(20.6–26.3%). That gap is what the encoder adds.

## What these numbers mean, and what they don't

- **They reproduce a published result on public data.** They say nothing yet about code typing,
  about our students, or about cheating. P3 starts that work.
- **The global-threshold EER is 2–3 times higher** than the per-subject average: 3.42% against
  1.17% at M = 70, G = 10, and 7.24% against 4.11% at G = 1. Our system uses one threshold
  (SPEC §8.5), so the global figures are the honest guide for us.
- **The spread between subjects is large.** At G = 1 the per-subject SD is 6.6 points, so a
  minority of typists is much harder to verify than the average suggests. That is a fairness
  point for the reviewer queue.
- **M = 50 and M = 70 are almost identical.** The data explains it: a sentence has a median of 46
  keystrokes, and only 14% reach 70, so M = 70 mostly adds padding. TypeNet's "plateau after 70"
  is a property of this dataset's sentence length, as our protocol argued. Continuous code
  typing fills every window, so the M ablation stays in P3.
- **Same-sitting comparisons.** All 15 sentences come from one online test, so every genuine
  comparison stays within one sitting. Our study separates sittings by at least a day and will
  be harder.
- **Identity, not authorship.** The genuine student typing someone else's code (A4, and A5
  typed) is untouched by these numbers.
- About 2.1% of D1 participants used small or on-screen keyboards. TypeNet's set includes them,
  and so does ours.

## Deviations that mattered

1. **Learning rate 0.005, not 0.05.** It was chosen on 1,000 validation subjects held out from
   the training subjects, before any test access (amended deviation 6, D-032).
   - With 0.05 the training loss *rose* over 200 epochs (0.81 → 1.21), and validation EER was 36.5%.
   - With 0.01 it was 2.31%, and with 0.005 it was 2.04%.
   - Whether this reflects a typo in the paper or a Keras/PyTorch difference is unknown.
2. **67,000 training subjects, not 68,000.** 1,000 were held out for learning-rate selection.
3. **No recurrent dropout** (deviation 3). It wasn't needed to reach the bands.
4. Everything else is as listed in p2_protocol §9. The spread over 5 random test draws
   (SD 0.07–0.31 points) shows that our choice of test subjects barely matters.

## Ablation and baselines

- **Prototype vs Eq. 5:** within 0.05 points per subject at every cell. The prototype rule has
  a slightly *lower* global EER at G ≥ 2 (for example 3.24% against 3.42% at M = 70, G = 10).
  Eq. 5 stays primary as pre-registered (D-031). The difference is too small to matter, and the
  prototype rule is cheaper online.
- **Digraph baseline (SPEC §7.2):** 24.7–26.3% EER. It barely improves with more enrollment, and
  at G = 1 it can score only **65%** of comparisons.
- **Sequence-statistics baseline:** 20.6–25.3% EER, always available.

## Process

- **Data:** `Keystrokes.zip` (sha256 `5fb217e0…88e270420`), read without unzipping. All 168,593
  participants were kept. Derived data and models are on `D:\integrity-data\derived\`, outside

- **Compute:** RTX 3050, about 80 minutes per M = 70 model and 51 minutes at M = 50. One cuDNN

  resumed run equals an uninterrupted one.

  - the OS-injection check, which found `isTrusted` is useless against OS-level auto-typers and
    that `timeStamp` is the input time;

  - the secrets moved out of the repo, with an encrypted backup.

## What works

- Every P2 component runs end to end: capture-side pairing, TypeNet matrices, both baselines,
  the encoder, the protocol and the EER, on real data.
- The reproduction is within every band, and it is reproducible: seeded, with checkpoints, a run
  manifest and a ledger.
- The encoder, the scorers and the EER code can be reused in P3 for our own data.

## What is weak

1. **The learning rate departs from the paper.** It is a documented, validation-based choice,
   but a reader comparing with TypeNet must be told.
2. **There is still no code-typing data.** D1 is short memorised English sentences, typed in one
   sitting. These EERs are an upper bound on what identity can give us.
3. **The real-keyboard check is still not run.** Overlaps, auto-repeat and laptop versus external
   keyboards need human typists. It is the one open item in P2's acceptance.
4. **The secrets backup is on the same SSD.** The offline USB copy is still open.
5. **Licence:** D1 is non-commercial research with attribution. Using models trained on it in
   patent-support documents needs your judgement.
6. ruff and mypy are still not installed, so they were not run.
